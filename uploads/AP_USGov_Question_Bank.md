## FRQ 4 — SCOTUS Comparison ★★★

**(A)** In ***Schenck v. United States* (1919)**, the Supreme Court established the "clear and present danger" test. Explain the facts of the case and the constitutional reasoning the Court used to uphold Schenck's conviction.

**(C)** A student argues that the two decisions are fundamentally inconsistent and that the Court should choose one principle to govern all First Amendment cases. Evaluate this argument using your knowledge of how courts apply constitutional precedent.

---

**Scoring Guidance:**

- **(A)** — Facts: Schenck distributed leaflets urging resistance to the military draft during WWI. Court upheld conviction; Holmes wrote that speech may be restricted when it creates a "clear and present danger" of substantial harm. Context (wartime) matters — the same speech may be protected in peacetime.
- **(B)** — *Tinker* extended First Amendment protection to symbolic student speech (armbands protesting Vietnam War) in a school setting. The Court held that students don't lose constitutional rights at the schoolhouse gate; speech can only be restricted if it causes "material and substantial disruption." Unlike *Schenck*, there was no direct threat of imminent harm — the Court required a much higher showing before allowing restriction.
- **(C)** — The student's argument oversimplifies First Amendment doctrine. The evolution from *Schenck* to *Tinker* reflects the Court refining doctrine in light of new circumstances — a normal and appropriate function of common law development. A single rule could either over-restrict speech (using *Schenck* broadly) or under-protect public safety (using *Tinker* broadly in all contexts).

**(A)** Explain the constitutional question in ***Marbury v. Madison* (1803)** and the significance of the Court's ruling for the balance of power among the three branches.

**(B)** Explain how the constitutional reasoning in ***McCulloch v. Maryland* (1819)** reinforced or extended the power of the federal government relative to the states.

**(C)** Both cases involved Chief Justice John Marshall. Explain how these two decisions together shaped the structural relationship between the three branches of the federal government and between the federal and state governments, making reference to at least **one** foundational document.

---

**Scoring Guidance:**

- **(A)** — Constitutional question: Whether the Supreme Court could compel Secretary of State Madison to deliver Marbury's commission. Ruling: The Court cannot use Section 13 of the Judiciary Act to issue writs because Section 13 is unconstitutional — it expanded the Court's original jurisdiction beyond Article III. Significance: Established **judicial review** — Court's power to strike down laws conflicting with the Constitution, making the judiciary a co-equal branch.
- **(B)** — Question: Whether Congress could charter a national bank and whether Maryland could tax it. Ruling: (1) Necessary and Proper Clause gives Congress implied powers — bank is constitutional; (2) States cannot tax federal instruments — Supremacy Clause prevails. Reinforced federal supremacy and broad interpretation of congressional power.
- **(C)** — Together: *Marbury* established the Court as the authoritative interpreter of the Constitution (checks legislative power); *McCulloch* established that the federal government's enumerated + implied powers are supreme over state authority. Foundational documents: Federalist No. 78 (Hamilton anticipated judicial review); Federalist No. 51 (branches must check each other); Article VI Supremacy Clause (federal law supreme). Together, Marshall cemented a strong federal government with the Supreme Court as its constitutional guardian.
